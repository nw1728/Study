Peaks, valleys, and how to check an area

Two things we untangled: what critical point, local and absolute max/min actually mean, and how to prove an integral answer is right before you trust it. Drag the dot. Move the endpoints. Watch which point wins.

Part 1 · the vocabulary

The wrong instinct, first

What everyone assumes

“The derivative is zero, so this is a maximum.” Nope. Gagal. Flat ground is only a suspect, not a verdict.

Gogo gaga version: imagine you are hiking with your eyes closed. Your feet can only feel the slope. When the ground goes flat, you might be standing on a hilltop, in the bottom of a valley, or on a flat landing halfway up a staircase that keeps going up. Your feet cannot tell the difference. You need one more test.

The one sentence that fixes it

The turunan \(f'(x)\) is not height. It is steepness. \(f(x)\) tells you how high you are; \(f'(x)\) tells you which way the ground tilts.

critical point

An \(x\) where \(f'(x)=0\) (flat ground) or \(f'(x)\) does not exist (a sharp corner or a cliff), and \(x\) is inside the interval.

Baby words: “a spot worth checking.” A suspect the police brought in. Not yet guilty.

local maximum

A point higher than everything in its own small neighbourhood. In symbols: \(f(c)\ge f(x)\) for all \(x\) near \(c\).

Baby words: “the top of this hill.” There may be a much taller mountain 5 km away. Doesn't matter here.

local minimum

A point lower than everything nearby: \(f(c)\le f(x)\) for \(x\) near \(c\).

Baby words: “the bottom of this valley.” Also called a relative minimum — same thing, different book.

absolute max / min

The single highest and single lowest value of \(f\) over the whole interval \([a,b]\). Also called global.

Baby words: “the champion.” One winner for highest, one for lowest, over the entire walk.

the two endpoints

\(x=a\) and \(x=b\) are candidates too, even though \(f'\) there is usually nowhere near zero.

Baby words: “where the walk starts and stops.” Forgetting these is mistake #1 on the exam. The wall of the room can be the highest point in it.

EVT

Extreme Value Theorem: if \(f\) is continuous on a closed interval \([a,b]\), then an absolute max and an absolute min are guaranteed to exist.

Baby words: “no holes, no open ends → a winner exists.” On \((0,1)\) or with a jump, the winner can escape.

The two recipes

First Derivative Test, then the candidate hunt

Is this critical point a hilltop or a valley?

Look at the sign of \(f'\) just left and just right of it. The flip is the whole answer.

left → rightmeaningit is a…
+ → −uphill then downhilllocal max
− → +downhill then uphilllocal min
+ → +uphill, brief pause, uphillneither
− → −downhill, pause, downhillneither

Try function f(x) = x³ in the tool above: \(f'(0)=0\), perfectly flat, yet it is neither a max nor a min. That is the counterexample that kills the wrong instinct.

Absolute max/min on \([a,b]\) — 5 steps

  1. Differentiate. Find \(f'(x)\).
  2. Solve \(f'(x)=0\). Also note where \(f'\) does not exist. Keep only the ones inside \((a,b)\) — a critical point outside the interval is not on your walk.
  3. Build the candidate list: those critical points plus \(a\) plus \(b\).
  4. Plug every candidate into \(f\), never into \(f'\). You want heights now, not slopes.
  5. Biggest value = absolute max. Smallest = absolute min. Done. No sign chart needed for this job.
Your four repeat traps

1. Forgetting the endpoints. In the very example below, the champion is an endpoint and the pretty local max loses 2 against 18.

2. Plugging candidates into \(f'\) instead of \(f\). You would be comparing steepness, then reporting it as height.

3. Assuming flat means extreme. Suspect, not verdict. Do the sign flip.

4. Ignoring the domain. For \(f(x)=\frac1x+\ln x\) nothing exists at \(x\le 0\), so \(x=0\) can never be a candidate.

From our own sessions

Worked examples, step by step

Find the absolute max and min of \(f(x)=x^3-3x\) on \([-1.5,\,3]\) 26 Sep session
  1. Differentiate. Power Rule on each piece: \(f'(x)=3x^2-3\). Factor it, because factored form hands you the roots for free: \(f'(x)=3(x-1)(x+1)\).
  2. Set it to zero. \(3(x-1)(x+1)=0\Rightarrow x=-1\) and \(x=1\). Both sit inside \((-1.5,3)\), so both stay. These are the two flat spots on the walk.
  3. Candidate list. \(x=-1.5\) (left wall), \(x=-1\), \(x=1\), \(x=3\) (right wall). Four suspects.
  4. Heights only — plug into \(f\): \[f(-1.5)=-3.375+4.5=1.125,\quad f(-1)=-1+3=2,\quad f(1)=1-3=-2,\quad f(3)=27-9=18\]
  5. Compare. Biggest is \(18\), smallest is \(-2\).
Answer Absolute maximum \(f=18\) at \(x=3\) — an endpoint. Absolute minimum \(f=-2\) at \(x=1\).

And the local story, from the sign chart: \(f'>0\) on \((-1.5,-1)\), \(f'<0\) on \((-1,1)\), \(f'>0\) on \((1,3)\). So \(x=-1\) is a local max (+ → −) with height 2, and \(x=1\) is a local min (− → +) with height −2.

✅ numerical check

We scanned 700 001 evenly spaced points across \([-1.5,3]\): the largest value found was \(18\) at \(x=3\), the smallest \(-2\) at \(x=1\). The algebra and the brute force agree.

The lesson of this one

The local max at \(x=-1\) looks like the answer on the graph — it is the only real hilltop. It loses anyway, 2 against 18, because the curve keeps climbing after the valley and the interval lets it climb all the way to \(x=3\). Local champion ≠ global champion.

Absolute extremes of \(f(x)=\frac1x+\ln x\) on \([0.5,\,4]\) Ex 39 · L02
  1. Domain first. \(\ln x\) needs \(x>0\) and \(\frac1x\) hates \(x=0\). The interval \([0.5,4]\) is safely positive, so we may continue.
  2. Differentiate. \(\frac1x=x^{-1}\) so its derivative is \(-x^{-2}\); the derivative of \(\ln x\) is \(\frac1x\). Together \(f'(x)=-\frac{1}{x^2}+\frac1x\).
  3. One denominator. Put both over \(x^2\): \(f'(x)=\dfrac{-1+x}{x^{2}}=\dfrac{x-1}{x^{2}}\). Only the pembilang can make a fraction zero. The penyebut only makes it undefined.
  4. Set numerator to zero. \(x-1=0\Rightarrow x=1\), inside \((0.5,4)\). Keep it.
  5. Candidates and heights. \[f(0.5)=2+\ln 0.5\approx 1.3069,\quad f(1)=1+0=1,\quad f(4)=0.25+\ln 4\approx 1.6363\]
Answer Absolute minimum \(f(1)=1\) at \(x=1\); absolute maximum \(f(4)=\tfrac14+\ln 4\approx 1.636\) at \(x=4\) — again an endpoint.

Sign check: \(x^2\) is always positive, so the sign of \(f'\) is just the sign of \(x-1\): negative before 1, positive after. − → + means \(x=1\) is a local min, and it happens to be the global one too.

The counterexample: \(f(x)=x^3\) on \([-1,\,1]\) — flat but neither why “suspect only”

\(f'(x)=3x^2\). Setting \(3x^2=0\) gives \(x=0\), so \(x=0\) is a genuine critical point: the tangent there is perfectly horizontal.

But \(3x^2\) is positive on both sides of 0 (squares can't be negative). So the sign pattern is + → +: no flip, no extremum. The path pauses for an instant and then continues uphill.

Answer \(x=0\) is critical but is neither a local max nor a local min. The absolute extremes are the two endpoints: max \(f(1)=1\), min \(f(-1)=-1\).

This shape has a name you will meet later: an inflection point with a horizontal tangent. Concavity flips there instead of direction.

Part 2 · trust nothing

Four ways to check an integral

The wrong instinct

“I finished the algebra, so the answer is right.” Integration has no built-in alarm. A wrong antiderivative produces a perfectly clean-looking number. So you check — every time, with at least one of these four.

method 1 · the king

Differentiate your answer

If \(F'(x)\) gives you back exactly the thing under the integral sign, you are right. No exceptions, no opinions.

Integrating and differentiating cancel each other out. This is the only check that proves the antiderivative.

method 2 · eyeball

Estimate the area

A definite integral is signed area. Sketch it roughly, count boxes. If your answer says 40 and the picture says “about 1”, stop.

Catches factor-of-10 and sign disasters instantly.

method 3 · squeeze

Bound it between two rectangles

If \(m\le f(x)\le M\) on \([a,b]\), then \(m(b-a)\le\displaystyle\int_a^b f\le M(b-a)\).

Two rectangles: the flattest one and the tallest one. The true area must hide between them.

method 4 · sign sanity

Which side of zero?

Integrand positive all the way across → the answer must be positive. Negative all the way → negative.

One second of thought, catches every dropped minus sign.

From our own sessions

The integrals we solved, and how we knew they were right

\(\displaystyle\int_0^1 x\,e^{x^2}\,dx\) — substitution, and why it is even possible Ex 35 · 17 Sep
Trap first

Instinct: “\(\int e^{ax}dx=\frac1a e^{ax}\), so divide by the inside.” That shortcut is only for a linear inside. Here the inside is \(x^2\), which is curved, so dividing is illegal: \(\frac{1}{x^2}e^{x^2}\) is not an antiderivative.

Worse: \(e^{x^2}\) on its own has no elementary antiderivative at all. The lonely \(x\) in front is the entire reason this problem is doable — it supplies the inner derivative that substitution needs.

  1. Name the inside. \(u=x^2\). Always the thing wrapped inside another function.
  2. Differentiate it. \(du=2x\,dx\), so \(x\,dx=\tfrac12\,du\). The \(\frac12\) pays back the 2 that the Chain Rule would have produced.
  3. Regroup and swap. \(x e^{x^2}dx=e^{x^2}(x\,dx)=\tfrac12 e^{u}du\). Every \(x\) is gone — that is the signal the substitution worked.
  4. Convert the limits. \(x=0\to u=0\), \(x=1\to u=1\). They happen to look unchanged only because \(0^2=0\) and \(1^2=1\). Not a rule.
  5. Integrate. \(\tfrac12\int_0^1 e^u du=\tfrac12\big[e^u\big]_0^1=\tfrac12(e-1)\).
Answer \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 x e^{x^2}dx=\frac{e-1}{2}\approx 0.8591409142\)

Check 1 (differentiate back): \(\frac{d}{dx}\left[\tfrac12 e^{x^2}\right]=\tfrac12 e^{x^2}\cdot 2x = x e^{x^2}\) ✅ original integrand recovered.
Check 3 (squeeze): on \([0,1]\) the integrand runs from 0 up to \(e\approx2.718\), so the area must lie in \([0,\,2.718]\). It does.
Check 2 (numbers): Simpson's rule gives \(0.8591409142295\) — matches to 10 decimals.

\(\displaystyle\int \sqrt{3-2s}\;ds\) — a linear inside, so the shortcut is legal Ex 17 · 19 Sep
  1. Ask the question. “Is there something inside something?” Yes: \(3-2s\) lives inside a square root. → substitution.
  2. Substitute. \(u=3-2s\), \(du=-2\,ds\), so \(ds=-\tfrac12 du\).
  3. Rewrite. \(\int u^{1/2}\left(-\tfrac12\right)du=-\tfrac12\cdot\frac{u^{3/2}}{3/2}=-\tfrac13 u^{3/2}\). Dividing by \(3/2\) means multiplying by \(2/3\) — that is where the \(\frac13\) comes from.
  4. Go home to \(s\), and never forget the \(+C\).
Answer \(\displaystyle\int\sqrt{3-2s}\,ds=-\frac13(3-2s)^{3/2}+C\)

Check (differentiate back): \(-\frac13\cdot\frac32(3-2s)^{1/2}\cdot(-2)=(3-2s)^{1/2}=\sqrt{3-2s}\) ✅. Watch the two minus signs cancelling — that is exactly the step that goes wrong when rushed.

\(\displaystyle\int \frac{dy}{(\arctan y)(1+y^2)}\) — the “derivative on top” pattern Ex 65 · 21 Sep

Pattern to memorise: when the derivative of a thing sits on top and the thing itself sits on the bottom, the answer is \(\ln\lvert\text{thing}\rvert\).

  1. Spot the pair. The derivative of \(\arctan y\) is \(\frac{1}{1+y^2}\) — and there it is, already in the integrand.
  2. Substitute. \(u=\arctan y\Rightarrow du=\dfrac{dy}{1+y^{2}}\).
  3. The integral collapses. \(\displaystyle\int\frac{du}{u}=\ln\lvert u\rvert + C\).
Answer \(\ln\lvert\arctan y\rvert + C\)

Check: \(\frac{d}{dy}\ln\lvert\arctan y\rvert=\frac{1}{\arctan y}\cdot\frac{1}{1+y^2}\) ✅ — the Chain Rule hands back the original fraction.

\(\displaystyle\int_2^4 \frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^2}\) — substitution with converted limits Ex 31 · 21 Sep
  1. Substitute. \(u=\ln x\Rightarrow du=\dfrac{dx}{x}\). The \(\frac{dx}{x}\) is sitting right there, so the swap is clean.
  2. Convert the limits (Theorem 7): \(x=2\to u=\ln 2\), \(x=4\to u=\ln 4\). Limits converted means you never go back to \(x\).
  3. Integrate. \(\displaystyle\int_{\ln 2}^{\ln 4}u^{-2}du=\Big[-\frac1u\Big]_{\ln 2}^{\ln 4}=\frac{1}{\ln 2}-\frac{1}{\ln 4}\).
  4. Simplify. \(\ln 4=2\ln 2\), so this is \(\frac{1}{\ln 2}-\frac{1}{2\ln 2}=\frac{1}{2\ln 2}\).
Answer \(\dfrac{1}{2\ln 2}\approx 0.7213475204\)

Check 4 (sign): on \([2,4]\) the integrand is positive, so the answer must be positive ✅. Check 3 (squeeze): the integrand falls from \(\approx1.041\) to \(\approx0.130\), width 2, so the area sits between \(0.26\) and \(2.08\) — and \(0.721\) does.

\(\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{4}{\sqrt{4-s^2}}\,ds\) — recognising the arcsin form Ex 41 · 21 Sep

Standard form to recognise on sight: \(\displaystyle\int\frac{ds}{\sqrt{a^2-s^2}}=\arcsin\frac{s}{a}+C\). Here \(a^2=4\), so \(a=2\).

  1. Pull the constant out. \(4\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{ds}{\sqrt{4-s^2}}\).
  2. Apply the form. \(4\Big[\arcsin\frac{s}{2}\Big]_0^1 = 4\left(\arcsin\tfrac12-\arcsin 0\right)\).
  3. Unit circle values. \(\arcsin\frac12=\frac{\pi}{6}\), \(\arcsin 0 = 0\).
  4. Multiply. \(4\cdot\frac{\pi}{6}=\frac{2\pi}{3}\).
Answer \(\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\approx 2.0943951024\)

Check 3 (squeeze): heights run from \(4/2=2\) to \(4/\sqrt3\approx2.309\) over a width of 1, so the area must be between 2 and 2.31 ✅ — \(2.094\) fits snugly.

Area between \(y=2x^2\) and \(y=x^4-2x^2\) 24 Sep
  1. Find where they meet. \(2x^2=x^4-2x^2\Rightarrow x^4-4x^2=0\Rightarrow x^2(x^2-4)=0\), so \(x=0,\,\pm2\).
  2. Decide who is on top. Test \(x=1\): \(2(1)=2\) versus \(1-2=-1\). The parabola \(2x^2\) is above. It stays above across the whole span.
  3. Integrate top minus bottom. \(\displaystyle\int_{-2}^{2}\big[2x^2-(x^4-2x^2)\big]dx=\int_{-2}^{2}\big(4x^2-x^4\big)dx\).
  4. Use symmetry. Both terms are even, so integrate on \([0,2]\) and double: \(2\Big[\frac{4x^3}{3}-\frac{x^5}{5}\Big]_0^2=2\Big(\frac{32}{3}-\frac{32}{5}\Big)\).
  5. Combine. \(\frac{32}{3}-\frac{32}{5}=32\cdot\frac{2}{15}=\frac{64}{15}\), doubled gives \(\frac{128}{15}\).
Answer \(\dfrac{128}{15}\approx 8.5333333\)

Check 4 (sign): area is positive ✅. Trap avoided: integrating \(x^4-2x^2\) minus \(2x^2\) instead would give \(-\frac{128}{15}\) — the same size with the wrong sign, which is the classic “who is on top” mistake.

Problem A · absolute max and min of \(f(x)=x^2-4x+1\) on \([0,\,5]\) set 26 Sep

Before you open this: did you write down three candidates, not one?

  1. Differentiate. \(f'(x)=2x-4\).
  2. Set to zero. \(2x-4=0\Rightarrow x=2\), inside \((0,5)\) ✅.
  3. Candidates. \(x=0\), \(x=2\), \(x=5\).
  4. Heights. \(f(0)=1\), \(f(2)=4-8+1=-3\), \(f(5)=25-20+1=6\).
  5. Compare. Largest 6, smallest −3.
Answer Absolute maximum \(f=6\) at \(x=5\) (endpoint). Absolute minimum \(f=-3\) at \(x=2\) (the critical point).

Sign chart: \(f'<0\) before \(x=2\), \(f'>0\) after → − → + → local min, which is also the global min. This is an upward parabola, so its vertex being the lowest point is no surprise; the max had to come from an endpoint, and the far one wins.

Problem B · evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^4\frac{x^2+x}{x^{3/2}}\,dx\) still open from 16 Sep

First ask the technique question: “is there something inside something?” No — it is a fraction with a single power on the bottom. So split it, don't substitute.

  1. Split the fraction. \(\dfrac{x^2}{x^{3/2}}+\dfrac{x}{x^{3/2}}\).
  2. Subtract exponents. \(x^{2-3/2}=x^{1/2}\) and \(x^{1-3/2}=x^{-1/2}\). So the integrand is \(\sqrt{x}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\).
  3. Power Rule on each. \(\int x^{1/2}dx=\frac{x^{3/2}}{3/2}=\frac23x^{3/2}\) and \(\int x^{-1/2}dx=\frac{x^{1/2}}{1/2}=2x^{1/2}\).
  4. Evaluate at 4 and 1. At \(x=4\): \(\frac23\cdot 8+2\cdot 2=\frac{16}{3}+4\). At \(x=1\): \(\frac23+2\).
  5. Subtract. \(\left(\frac{16}{3}+4\right)-\left(\frac23+2\right)=\frac{14}{3}+2=\frac{20}{3}\).
Answer \(\dfrac{20}{3}\approx 6.6666667\)

Check 3 (squeeze): on \([1,4]\) the integrand \(\sqrt x+\frac{1}{\sqrt x}\) runs from \(2\) (at \(x=1\)) to \(2.5\) (at \(x=4\)), width 3 → area between 6 and 7.5. \(6.667\) fits ✅. Check 1: differentiate \(\frac23x^{3/2}+2x^{1/2}\) → \(x^{1/2}+x^{-1/2}\) ✅.
